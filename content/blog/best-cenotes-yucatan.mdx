We named our company after them, so you will forgive us for having opinions. Cenotes are the freshwater sinkholes that dot the flat limestone of the Yucatán peninsula, thousands of them, formed where the porous rock collapsed into the underground rivers below. Many sit around the rim of the Chicxulub crater, left by the asteroid that ended the age of the dinosaurs. For the ancient Maya they were the only reliable source of water in a land with almost no rivers on the surface, and sacred portals to Xibalbá, the underworld.

Swimming in one is unlike any other kind of swim. The water is cool, around 24 to 25 degrees all year, and often so clear you can see fish moving ten metres below. Here is how we think about them, and the ones we keep coming back to.

## The three types of cenote

Not all cenotes are alike, and knowing the difference helps you choose.

- **Open cenotes** have collapsed fully and look like natural pools or small lakes, open to the sky. They are bright, easy to swim in and good for families.
- **Semi-open cenotes** are partly roofed by rock, so you swim from daylight into shade under an overhang. Often the most dramatic for photographs.
- **Cave cenotes** are enclosed caverns, entered through a small hole in the ground or by stairs cut into the rock. Shafts of light fall through openings in the ceiling, and roots dangle down to the water. They are the oldest, and they feel it.

## Near Valladolid

Valladolid is our favourite base for cenotes. Within half an hour you can reach some of the most beautiful in the peninsula, and the town itself has one in the middle of it.

### Cenote Suytun

Suytun is the cave cenote you have probably already seen on your phone: a round stone platform reached by a narrow walkway, with a single shaft of light falling through a hole in the roof onto the water. That beam is strongest around the middle of the day, and that is exactly when the queue for the platform photo is longest. Our advice is to go at opening, accept a softer light, and have the cavern almost to yourself. The water around the platform is shallow-ish and calm; a life jacket is required.

### Cenotes Samulá and Xkeken

These two sit side by side at **Dzitnup**, a ten-minute drive west of Valladolid, and share a ticket. **Xkeken** is a cave cenote dripping with stalactites, lit by a small opening in the roof. **Samulá** is our favourite of the pair: a vast domed cavern with a single round hole in the ceiling, through which the roots of a tree reach all the way down to the water. Around midday a column of light hits the surface, turning it electric blue.

### Cenote Zací

Right in the centre of Valladolid, Zací is a large semi-open cenote with a rock overhang curving above the dark green water, swallows darting in and out. It is less polished than some, cheaper, and loved by local families, especially on weekend afternoons. Brave swimmers jump from the ledge. There is a restaurant at the top serving decent Yucatecan dishes.

### Cenote Ik Kil

Ik Kil is an open cenote just a few minutes from Chichén Itzá, a near-perfect circle of deep blue water around 40 metres below ground level, with vines and small waterfalls trailing down the walls. It is genuinely stunning, and genuinely busy, as many tour buses stop here for lunch after the ruins. Arrive when it opens, or late in the afternoon, and it becomes a different place.

## Near Tulum

The cenotes around Tulum are part of some of the longest underwater cave systems ever mapped, including Sac Actun, which runs for more than 300 kilometres. That makes this the world capital of cavern and cave diving, but snorkellers get plenty too.

### Dos Ojos

Dos Ojos, the "two eyes", is a pair of connected cenotes north of Tulum, linked by a cavern passage. Snorkelling here is a gentle introduction to the cave world: the water is astonishingly clear, and with a torch you can follow the rock formations into the shadows. For certified divers, the guided cavern dives here are some of the most famous on earth, with light pouring through the water like stained glass.

### Gran Cenote

On the road from Tulum to Cobá, Gran Cenote is a semi-open cenote with wooden walkways, small caverns to snorkel through and resident turtles grazing in the shallows. It is beautiful and very popular, so again, the earlier the better. Bring a mask; the underwater views beneath the rock overhangs are the highlight.

## South of Mérida

The countryside south and east of Mérida feels a world away from the Riviera Maya. Here the cenotes come wrapped in the history of the henequen haciendas that once dominated the region.

### Hacienda Mucuyché

About 50 minutes south of Mérida, near Abalá, this restored hacienda has two cenotes in its grounds. **Cenote Carlota**, named after the Empress Carlota who reputedly bathed here in the 1860s, is a cave cenote reached along an old stone canal, and **Cenote Azul Maya** is a cavern of brilliant blue water. Visits are guided and timed, which keeps numbers sensible, and the walk through the hacienda is a lovely bonus.

### The cenotes of Cuzamá

Near the village of Cuzamá, three cave cenotes are reached in the most charming way: on a **truck**, a small horse-drawn cart that runs along the narrow rails once used to carry henequen fibre. You rattle through the scrub between stops, climb down ladders into each cavern, and swim in water so clear the bottom looks within reach. It is rustic, a bit bumpy, and one of the most memorable half-days in the Yucatán. The ladders are steep, so it suits confident swimmers.

## How to time your visit

Light and crowds are everything. Most cenotes open around 8 or 9 in the morning and close by late afternoon. The first hour is almost always the quietest, and the water is at its stillest. In cave cenotes, the famous light shafts appear when the sun is high, from roughly 11am to 2pm, so if photography matters more than solitude, plan around that.

Weekdays are calmer than weekends, when local families rightly come out to enjoy their own cenotes. In the hottest months, April and May, a cenote is the best place in the Yucatán to be at 2pm. During the rainy season, from around June to October, visibility in open cenotes can dip slightly, but cave cenotes are largely unaffected.

## Cenote etiquette

Cenotes are fragile ecosystems and, for many Maya communities, sacred places. They are also connected to the aquifer that supplies the region's drinking water, so what washes off your skin does not just disappear.

- **Shower before you swim.** Almost every cenote has showers at the entrance, and using them is mandatory, not optional.
- **No sunscreen, insect repellent or lotions.** Many cenotes ban them entirely, including "biodegradable" ones. Put sunscreen on after your swim if you need it, and wear a rash vest for open cenotes instead.
- **Do not touch the formations.** Stalactites take thousands of years to grow, and oils from your hands stop them.
- **Wear the life jacket if asked.** Many cenotes now require them, and the water is often much deeper than it looks.
- **Bring cash.** Entry fees are usually paid in pesos, and many cenotes run by local cooperatives do not take cards. Lockers and life jacket hire may cost extra.
- **Keep your voice down.** Sound carries in a cavern, and the quiet is half the experience.

## A few practical notes

Pack water shoes, as steps can be slippery and limestone is sharp. Bring a quick-dry towel and a waterproof bag for your phone. Many cenotes have basic changing rooms, and some have small restaurants serving home-cooked food. And go with an open mind about the less famous ones. Some of our happiest afternoons have been spent in nameless village cenotes, sharing the water with a few kids from the next village and a lot of small black catfish.
