Ask most people to picture Mexico and they will describe a sun lounger in Cancún. We understand why. The Caribbean coast is genuinely beautiful, and there is nothing wrong with a week of turquoise water and a cold michelada. But it is a little like judging France by a single beach in Nice. Mexico is the size of Western Europe, with snow-capped volcanoes, high colonial cities, cloud forests, deserts full of cactus, two very different oceans and a capital that has been one of the great cities of the world for seven centuries.

We have spent years travelling it, for work and for no reason at all, and our specialists still find corners that surprise them. This guide is our honest, personal answer to the question we hear most: where should I actually go? It is not a ranking. Each place earns its spot for different reasons, and the right trip is always the one built around what you love. But if you want to get below the surface of this country, these are the places we keep sending people, and keep going back to ourselves.

## 1. Mexico City (CDMX)

*A capital of 22 million people that somehow feels like a collection of villages.*

**Best for:** food lovers, museum lovers, design and architecture fans, anyone who likes a city with real energy.

**Key highlights:** the Museo Nacional de Antropología, Roma and Condesa, the Centro Histórico, Coyoacán, a day trip to Teotihuacán.

If we could only send you to one place in Mexico, it might be here. Mexico City is loud, green, sprawling and endlessly generous. Mornings in **Condesa** start with a coffee under the jacarandas of **Parque México**, where dog walkers lead eight or nine dogs at once along the old horse-racing oval of Avenida Amsterdam. By lunchtime you are in **Roma Norte**, queueing for the tuna tostadas at **Contramar**, a long and noisy seafood lunch that is a city institution and rarely finishes before five.

The **Centro Histórico** is where the city's layers show. The excavated **Templo Mayor**, heart of Aztec Tenochtitlan, sits right beside the cathedral, which was built partly with its stones. A short walk away, **Palacio Nacional** holds Diego Rivera's murals of Mexican history, and the Art Nouveau **Palacio de Bellas Artes** is worth seeing at dusk when the light catches its marble. Give the **Museo Nacional de Antropología** in Chapultepec a full morning at least; the Aztec Sun Stone alone deserves twenty minutes. Note that it, like most of the city's museums, is closed on Mondays.

In the south, cobbled **Coyoacán** is calmer, and home to Frida Kahlo's **Casa Azul**, which you should book well in advance. Spend the afternoon eating tostadas at the **Mercado de Coyoacán** and wandering the plazas. And do take the hour's drive north to **Teotihuacán**, ideally at opening time, before the heat and the tour buses arrive. Watching the Avenue of the Dead stretch out from the Pyramid of the Moon is one of those moments that rearranges your sense of history.

One honest note: at 2,240 metres the altitude is real. Take your first afternoon slowly, drink plenty of water, and expect that second mezcal to land harder than usual.

## 2. Oaxaca

*Mole, mezcal, markets and weavers: Mexico's soul in one valley.*

**Best for:** food and drink lovers, craft and textile fans, culture seekers, slow travellers.

**Key highlights:** the seven moles, mezcal palenques in Santiago Matatlán, Monte Albán, Hierve el Agua, weaving villages of the Central Valleys.

Oaxaca City is where many of our team fell properly in love with Mexico. It is small enough to walk, painted in ochre and green stone, and it takes its food more seriously than almost anywhere on earth. Start at the **Mercado 20 de Noviembre**, where the **pasillo de humo**, the smoke corridor, is lined with grills: you choose your raw meat, it is cooked in front of you, and you carry it to a table with tortillas, onions and chiles. Later, try a proper **mole negro**, dark with chocolate and burnt chiles, and a **tlayuda** from a street stall after dark.

Above the city, the Zapotec ruins of **Monte Albán** sit on a flattened mountaintop with views across three valleys. Go early, when the mist is still lifting. Further out, **Hierve el Agua** has mineral springs that have built what look like frozen waterfalls down the cliff face, and the village of **Teotitlán del Valle** is home to families who have woven rugs with natural dyes, cochineal red and indigo blue, for generations.

Then there is mezcal. In **Santiago Matatlán** and the villages around it, small family palenques still roast agave hearts in earth pits and crush them with a stone wheel pulled by a horse. Tasting a wild tobalá or tepextate with the person who distilled it, sitting on an upturned crate beside the still, is a world away from a shot in a bar.

Come in late July for the **Guelaguetza** festival of regional dance, or around 1 and 2 November for **Día de Muertos**, when the city is at its most moving. Book far ahead for both.

## 3. Mérida and the Yucatán haciendas

*The elegant, slightly sleepy capital of the Maya world.*

**Best for:** culture, architecture, food, couples, travellers who want the Yucatán without the beach crowds.

**Key highlights:** Paseo de Montejo, cochinita pibil, Uxmal and the Ruta Puuc, restored henequen haciendas, the flamingos of Celestún.

Mérida is the city we recommend to people who think they want Tulum but actually want something with more depth. It is a proper working city, the capital of Yucatán state, with pastel colonial streets, grand mansions built on the wealth of the henequen fibre boom along **Paseo de Montejo**, and a cultural life that spills into the streets every night. On Sundays the centre closes to traffic and families dance to live bands in the **Plaza Grande**.

The food is distinct from the rest of Mexico, shaped by Maya ingredients and old trade links with the Caribbean. Order **cochinita pibil**, pork slow-cooked in achiote and sour orange, ideally on a Sunday morning when the stalls are freshest, and try **sopa de lima** and **papadzules**. The central **Mercado Lucas de Gálvez** is chaotic and wonderful.

An hour south lies **Uxmal**, our favourite of the big Maya sites, with its curved Pyramid of the Magician and intricately carved Puuc-style facades. Fewer people visit than Chichén Itzá, and it feels all the better for it. The surrounding countryside is dotted with old **haciendas**, many now restored as quietly luxurious hotels with pools in the former machine houses. Staying in one for a night or two is one of the great pleasures of the Yucatán.

Be honest with yourself about the heat: April and May regularly reach 40 degrees Celsius. November to February is much kinder.

## 4. Valladolid and the cenote country

*A colonial town at the centre of the cenote belt.*

**Best for:** cenote swimmers, early risers, Chichén Itzá without the day-trip crush, families.

**Key highlights:** Cenote Zací, Calzada de los Frailes, Chichén Itzá at opening, Ek Balam, Cenotes Suytun and Samulá.

Valladolid is small, colourful and refreshingly normal. Its main square fills in the evening with families eating marquesitas, crisp rolled crêpes filled with Edam cheese and cajeta, and its prettiest street, the **Calzada de los Frailes**, leads down to the 16th-century **Convento de San Bernardino de Siena**.

The real reason to stay here, though, is location. Valladolid sits about 45 minutes from **Chichén Itzá**, which means you can be at the gates when they open, before the coaches from Cancún arrive at around ten. It is also surrounded by cenotes: **Zací** is right in town, the cave cenotes of **Samulá** and **Xkeken** are a ten-minute drive away, and **Suytun**, with its beam of midday light falling onto a stone platform, is just east. The partly restored city of **Ek Balam**, with remarkably preserved stucco carvings, is a short drive north.

A day further north to the flamingos of **Río Lagartos** and the pink salt flats of **Las Coloradas** makes a good extension, though we would gently warn that the pink is best photographed rather than swum in.

## 5. Tulum

*Jungle, ruins and a beach that is still beautiful, if you know where to stand.*

**Best for:** beach lovers, wellness, cenote diving, design-led hotels.

**Key highlights:** the clifftop ruins of Tulum, Gran Cenote and Dos Ojos, Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve, Cobá.

Tulum has changed enormously in the last decade, and we will not pretend otherwise. The beach road is busier and pricier than it was, and the town has grown fast. But the setting is still extraordinary. The **ruins of Tulum**, a walled Maya port perched on a cliff above Caribbean water, are one of the most photographed sights in the country for good reason. Go at opening; by eleven it is hot and crowded. The ruins and much of the nearby coast now sit within the **Jaguar National Park**, which charges an entry fee.

The magic of Tulum for us is in its surroundings. **Gran Cenote** and **Dos Ojos** are some of the best snorkelling cenotes in Mexico, part of an underground river system that runs for hundreds of kilometres. South of town, the **Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve** is a UNESCO-listed wilderness of lagoons, mangroves and ancient canals where we have seen dolphins, manatee trails and nesting turtles. Inland, the ruins of **Cobá** are still wrapped in jungle; you can hire a bike to ride between the temples.

The honest trade-off is **sargassum**, the seaweed that washes up along the Caribbean coast, mostly between roughly April and September. Some weeks are clear, some are not. A specialist can tell you which beaches are holding up.

## 6. Bacalar

*The lagoon of seven colours, and the calm the coast used to have.*

**Best for:** couples, kayakers and paddleboarders, slow travel, anyone escaping Tulum's crowds.

**Key highlights:** Laguna de Bacalar at sunrise, the stromatolites, Los Rápidos, Cenote Azul, the old pirate fort.

About two and a half hours south of Tulum, Bacalar is a freshwater lagoon over 40 kilometres long, its water shifting from pale turquoise to deep indigo as the bottom falls away into sinkholes. Paddling out at sunrise, before the wind picks up, on glass-still water with only herons for company, is one of our favourite mornings anywhere in Mexico.

The lagoon is also home to **stromatolites**, some of the oldest living structures on earth, so please do not stand on them. Drift with the current at **Los Rápidos**, swim the dark depths of **Cenote Azul** on the edge of town, and visit the **Fuerte de San Felipe**, built to defend against pirates in the 18th century. Bacalar is quieter and less polished than the coast, and that is exactly the point.

## 7. Holbox

*A car-free island of sand streets, flamingos and whale sharks.*

**Best for:** barefoot island time, wildlife, couples, families with older children.

**Key highlights:** swimming with whale sharks in summer, Punta Mosquito sandbar, flamingos, bioluminescence, sunset at Punta Coco.

Holbox sits off the northern tip of the Yucatán peninsula, reached by ferry from the little port of **Chiquilá**. There are no cars, just golf carts and bicycles on sandy streets lined with murals. The water on the north shore is shallow, calm and milky green, more lagoon than Caribbean postcard, and you can wade for hundreds of metres to the sandbar at **Punta Mosquito**, where flamingos sometimes feed.

From roughly June to mid-September, huge numbers of **whale sharks** gather offshore to feed on plankton, and swimming alongside the largest fish in the sea is something people talk about for the rest of their lives. Choose a responsible operator who follows the rules: small groups, no touching, and limited time in the water. On moonless nights, the shallows near the island glow with **bioluminescent plankton** when you move your hands through the water.

Holbox is small and simple, and the streets can flood after heavy rain. Come for the slowness.

## 8. San Miguel de Allende

*A rose-gold colonial town in the highlands, made for walking and long lunches.*

**Best for:** couples, art and design lovers, foodies, celebrations.

**Key highlights:** the Parroquia de San Miguel Arcángel, rooftop terraces at sunset, Fábrica La Aurora, hot springs, the Sanctuary of Atotonilco.

San Miguel is almost absurdly pretty. Its cobbled streets climb between houses painted saffron, terracotta and pink, and at the centre stands the **Parroquia de San Miguel Arcángel**, a neo-Gothic fantasy of pink stone that looks lit from within at golden hour. The best way to see it is from a rooftop terrace with a glass of local wine; vineyards around San Miguel and Dolores Hidalgo produce some genuinely good bottles now.

The town has a long history as an artists' colony and a large international community, which brings excellent restaurants and galleries. Spend a morning at **Fábrica La Aurora**, a former textile mill full of studios, and an afternoon soaking at the thermal pools of **La Gruta** on the road to Dolores. Just outside town, the **Sanctuary of Atotonilco**, whose painted ceilings have earned it the nickname of the Sistine Chapel of Mexico, is listed by UNESCO together with the town.

At about 1,900 metres, evenings are cool even in summer. Pack a layer.

## 9. Guanajuato

*A university town folded into a ravine, all colour, music and tunnels.*

**Best for:** culture, students of history, lively evenings, photographers.

**Key highlights:** the callejones, Callejón del Beso, Teatro Juárez, the funicular to El Pípila, a callejoneada with the estudiantinas.

An hour from San Miguel, Guanajuato could not be more different in mood. Built on the silver that made it one of the richest cities in the Spanish empire, it is packed into a narrow valley, with houses in every colour stacked up the slopes and a network of old tunnels running under the centre that now carry the traffic.

Lose yourself in the tiny **callejones**, the alleys, including the **Callejón del Beso**, so narrow that lovers on opposite balconies could kiss. Ride the **funicular** up to the monument of **El Pípila** for the view, which is best just before sunset. In the evening, join a **callejoneada**, where student musicians in Renaissance costume lead a crowd through the alleys, singing and telling stories. Every October the city hosts the **Festival Internacional Cervantino**, one of Latin America's biggest arts festivals. Diego Rivera was born here, and his childhood home is now a museum.

## 10. Chiapas

*Highland markets, Maya communities and temples swallowed by rainforest.*

**Best for:** adventurous travellers, culture, nature, photographers who are respectful.

**Key highlights:** San Cristóbal de las Casas, Sumidero Canyon, Palenque, the waterfalls of Misol-Ha and Agua Azul, Indigenous highland villages.

Chiapas is Mexico's southernmost state and, for many travellers, its most rewarding. **San Cristóbal de las Casas** sits at around 2,200 metres, a colonial town of low tiled roofs, cool mornings and woodsmoke, with a strong Indigenous Tzotzil and Tzeltal presence. Visit the nearby village of **San Juan Chamula**, whose church has no pews, a floor of pine needles and a ritual life all its own. Photography inside is strictly forbidden, and you should ask before photographing anyone outside.

From the town of Chiapa de Corzo, boats run through the **Sumidero Canyon**, whose walls rise around a kilometre above the river. Then head down into the lowland jungle to **Palenque**, our favourite Maya site of all. Its temples emerge from the rainforest to the roar of howler monkeys, and the Temple of the Inscriptions held the tomb of the great king Pakal. On the way, stop at the waterfalls of **Misol-Ha** and **Agua Azul**, though the latter can turn brown after heavy rain.

An honest word: road travel in parts of Chiapas can be disrupted by blockades, and conditions change. This is a region where travelling with local guides and a specialist keeping an eye on the latest news really matters.

## 11. Puerto Escondido

*Big Pacific waves, bare feet and the best sunsets on the coast.*

**Best for:** surfers, beach lovers on a budget or not, young couples, wildlife.

**Key highlights:** Zicatela beach, Playa Carrizalillo, bioluminescent Laguna de Manialtepec, Mazunte and Zipolite, turtle releases.

On the Oaxacan coast, Puerto Escondido has a very different energy from the Caribbean. The water is deep blue, the sand is golden, and the waves are serious. **Zicatela**, the famous Mexican Pipeline, draws big-wave surfers from around the world, and watching them from the beach with a cold drink at sunset is entertainment enough. Swimmers should stay well away; the currents there are dangerous.

For gentler water, walk down the steps to **Playa Carrizalillo**, a small horseshoe bay perfect for beginner surf lessons. At night, take a boat out on the **Laguna de Manialtepec**, where the water lights up with bioluminescence around every paddle stroke. A short drive down the coast leads to the laid-back villages of **Mazunte** and **San Agustinillo**, and from late in the year there are sea turtle hatchling releases at sunset.

It combines beautifully with Oaxaca City. The short flight is easiest, and the new highway has made the drive far faster than the old mountain road.

## 12. The Copper Canyon

*A canyon system larger and in places deeper than the Grand Canyon, crossed by one of the world's great train rides.*

**Best for:** rail lovers, hikers, landscape photographers, travellers who have done Mexico's highlights.

**Key highlights:** El Chepe train, Divisadero viewpoint, the cable car at the adventure park, Rarámuri culture, the descent to Urique.

In the Sierra Tarahumara of Chihuahua, a series of canyons cut through the mountains on a scale that is hard to believe until you are standing at the rim. The best way in is aboard **El Chepe**, the train that climbs from the coast at Los Mochis up through dozens of tunnels and over towering bridges into pine forest. The **Chepe Express** offers a more comfortable, panoramic version on the western section.

Break the journey at **Divisadero**, where the train pauses at the edge of the canyon and you can look across three canyons at once. Stay at least a night: the sunsets here are some of the most spectacular we have seen in Mexico, and the cable car and zip lines at the adventure park offer something even bolder. This is the homeland of the **Rarámuri**, famous for long-distance running, and many families sell beautifully woven baskets at the stations.

The region is remote, and safety depends on staying within established routes. We only send travellers here with trusted local partners on the ground.

## Bonus: places on our own list

These are the places our team talks about over dinner, where we have been once and keep wanting to go back, or have been planning to go for years.

### Valle de Guadalupe, Baja California

Around 90 minutes south of the US border, this valley near **Ensenada** has become Mexico's most exciting wine region. Dusty roads lead to wineries built from reclaimed wood and stone, many with outdoor restaurants cooking over open fires. It is relaxed, slightly wild and produces some excellent Nebbiolo and Tempranillo. The **Fiestas de la Vendimia**, the harvest festival, takes place in August. Combine it with fish tacos in Ensenada and the whale-watching lagoons further south in winter.

### Real de Catorce, San Luis Potosí

A former silver-mining town high in the desert, reached through a single-lane tunnel more than two kilometres long. Half-abandoned, with ruined mansions and cobbled streets, it has an atmosphere unlike anywhere else in Mexico. The surrounding desert of **Wirikuta** is sacred to the Wixárika people, who make pilgrimages here, and should be treated with real respect. Ride out on horseback to the ghost town above at sunset and you will understand why so many people find it hard to leave.

## Practical tips for travelling Mexico

### Getting around

Mexico is big, and travel times catch people out. Domestic flights on airlines such as Aeroméxico, Volaris and Viva Aerobus are often the smartest way to link regions; Mexico City to Oaxaca takes about an hour by air against six or more by road. Long-distance buses, especially the first-class ADO network, are comfortable and reliable. In the Yucatán, the new **Tren Maya** connects Cancún, Mérida, Valladolid, Tulum, Bacalar and Palenque, though a private driver remains far more flexible for cenotes and ruins. In cities, Uber and DiDi work well and are generally safer and simpler than hailing taxis on the street.

### When to go

The dry season, roughly November to April, is the classic time for most of the country, with clear skies and comfortable temperatures. It is also the busiest and most expensive, particularly over Christmas, New Year and Semana Santa, the week before Easter. The rainy season, from around June to October, usually means a dramatic afternoon storm rather than all-day rain, and the landscapes are lush and green. Hurricane season on both coasts runs from about June to November, with the highest risk in September and early October. For a fuller breakdown, see our [month-by-month guide](/blog/when-to-visit-mexico).

### Money

The peso is the currency, and cards are widely accepted in hotels, restaurants and shops in cities and resort areas. Carry cash for markets, street food, small towns, cenote entrance fees and tips. Use ATMs inside banks or shopping centres rather than standalone machines on the street, and always choose to be charged in pesos rather than your home currency. A tip of 10 to 15 percent is standard in restaurants.

### Safety

We will be straightforward. Mexico has real security problems, and they are concentrated in specific places, many of which are far from the routes travellers take. The vast majority of visitors have no trouble at all. Check your government's travel advice, which is set state by state, and take it seriously. Use app-based taxis, avoid driving long distances at night, keep valuables discreet, and take local advice on where to go after dark. Do not drink tap water. Having a specialist who follows the situation on the ground, and adjusts your route if something changes, makes a genuine difference.
