Mexico City has a way of wrong-footing first-time visitors. People arrive expecting chaos and find tree-lined avenues, art deco apartment blocks, quiet plazas and some of the best restaurants on the continent. They also find genuine scale: more than 20 million people in the metropolitan area, traffic that can turn a 20-minute ride into an hour, and enough museums to fill a month.

The trick is to treat it as a collection of neighbourhoods rather than one giant city. Here is what we tell friends before their first visit.

## First, the altitude

The city sits at about 2,240 metres above sea level, higher than many ski resorts. Most people feel it only mildly: a little breathless on stairs, a light headache, poorer sleep for the first night or two. Take your first afternoon slowly, drink more water than you think you need, and go easy on alcohol, which hits harder up here. The sun is also stronger than it feels, so wear a hat even on cool days.

The air quality can be poor on still days in the dry season, from around February to May. It is worth checking if you have asthma.

## Where to stay

### Roma Norte

Our usual first recommendation. Roma is walkable and leafy, full of early 20th-century mansions, independent coffee shops, galleries and restaurants for every budget. Streets like **Colima** and **Álvaro Obregón** are lively from breakfast until late, and **Plaza Río de Janeiro**, with its copy of Michelangelo's David, is a lovely place to sit. Expect some nightlife noise on the busier blocks.

### Condesa

Next door and slightly calmer, Condesa is built around two parks, **Parque México** and **Parque España**. Its signature street, **Avenida Amsterdam**, follows the oval of an old horse-racing track and is shaded by huge trees, perfect for a morning run or walk. It is especially good for families and anyone who wants green space on the doorstep.

### Centro Histórico

The historic centre is where the big sights are, and staying here means you can see the **Zócalo** before the crowds. It is grand and atmospheric by day, though some streets get very quiet at night. We suggest it for one or two nights rather than a whole stay.

### Polanco

The city's polished, upscale district, with designer shops, international hotels and some of Mexico's most celebrated restaurants. It sits right beside **Chapultepec Park** and the Anthropology museum. Polished and safe, though a little less characterful than Roma or Condesa.

### Coyoacán

A former village in the south, with cobbled streets, colonial plazas and a bohemian, slower feel. It is lovely but a long ride from the centre, so we tend to recommend it as a day out rather than a base.

## Getting around

**Uber and DiDi** are the easiest way for most visitors to get around. They are inexpensive, widely available and safer than hailing taxis on the street. At the airport, use the authorised taxi booths inside the terminal or the app pick-up points.

The **Metro** is fast and astonishingly cheap, but very crowded at rush hour; the first carriages are often reserved for women and children at peak times. Walking is the best way to explore within a neighbourhood. On Sunday mornings, a long stretch of **Paseo de la Reforma** closes to cars for cyclists and walkers, and you can rent a city bike to join in.

Allow plenty of time for traffic, especially on weekday afternoons and when it rains. Plan your days by area, not by a list of sights.

## What to eat

The food is the reason many people come, and rightly so.

- **Tacos al pastor**, spit-roasted pork with pineapple, shaved onto small corn tortillas. Look for a busy stand with a big, glowing trompo.
- **Tacos de canasta**, steamed "basket tacos" sold from bicycles in the morning, filled with potato, beans or chicharrón.
- **Tamales and atole** for breakfast, from street vendors near Metro stations.
- **Seafood at Contramar** in Roma, especially the tuna tostadas and the pescado a la talla, half red, half green. Book ahead, and go for a long lunch.
- **Churros and hot chocolate at El Moro**, open since 1935.
- **Markets**: **Mercado de Medellín** in Roma for regional ingredients, **Mercado de San Juan** for exotic produce and tasting plates, and **Mercado de Coyoacán** for tostadas piled high.

Eating street food is a big part of the joy here. Choose stands with a queue of locals and a high turnover. Do not drink tap water, but ice in restaurants and established cafes is made from purified water.

Mexicans eat late. Lunch, the main meal of the day, typically starts around 2 or 3pm, and dinner rarely before 8pm.

## Museums and sights

The **Museo Nacional de Antropología** is one of the great museums of the world. Give it at least three hours; the Mexica hall with the Sun Stone and the Maya hall are the highlights. Like most museums in the city, it is **closed on Mondays**, which catches out a lot of travellers. The same goes for Frida Kahlo's **Casa Azul** in Coyoacán, which needs to be booked online, often weeks in advance.

In the centre, see the excavated **Templo Mayor**, Diego Rivera's murals at the **Palacio Nacional** (bring photo ID), and the **Palacio de Bellas Artes**, ideally with a ticket to the Ballet Folklórico. The **Casa de los Azulejos**, a palace covered in blue and white tiles, is a short walk away.

Other favourites: the **Museo Soumaya** and **Museo Jumex** in Polanco, the castle in **Chapultepec Park** with its sweeping view down Reforma, and the **Museo Casa de León Trotsky** in Coyoacán. Sunday is the busiest day, as residents get free entry to many museums.

For something entirely different, catch a night of **lucha libre** at **Arena México**, wrestling with masks, costumes and a crowd that makes it unforgettable.

## Xochimilco

The canals of Xochimilco, in the far south, are the last remnants of the lake system the Aztecs farmed. You hire a brightly painted **trajinera** boat and drift past floating mariachi bands and food vendors. At weekends it is a riotous party; on a weekday morning it is gentle and much quieter. Both are fun, just very different.

## Day trip: Teotihuacán

About an hour north-east of the city, **Teotihuacán** was one of the largest cities in the world around 1,500 years ago, long before the Aztecs. Walk the **Avenue of the Dead** between the **Pyramid of the Sun** and the **Pyramid of the Moon**, and see the carved serpent heads of the **Temple of the Feathered Serpent**. Climbing the pyramids has been restricted in recent years, so do not plan around it.

Go early. Get there for opening, before the tour groups and the midday heat, and bring a hat, sunscreen and water as there is little shade. For a real treat, a **hot-air balloon flight** over the site at dawn is spectacular, and a private guide makes a big difference in understanding what you are seeing.

## Staying safe

Mexico City is a huge city with the usual big-city issues. In the areas most visitors spend time, such as Roma, Condesa, Polanco, Coyoacán and the centre by day, the main things to watch are pickpockets and phone snatching in crowds. Use app taxis, especially at night, keep your phone close on busy streets, and take local advice about where to go after dark. You may hear the seismic alarm system being tested; if you hear it for real, follow the people around you.

## How many days?

We would say four days at a minimum for a first visit, and five or six if you want to include Teotihuacán, Xochimilco and a slower pace.
